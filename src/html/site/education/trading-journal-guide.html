Trading Journal Guide: Track Your Way to Funded Status

The single habit that separates funded traders from everyone else — and how to build it right.

March 2026 · 9 min read

Why a Trading Journal Changes Everything

Ask any consistently profitable trader what made the biggest difference in their career and the answer is almost always the same: keeping a journal. Not indicators. Not a secret strategy. A disciplined record of every trade, every decision, and every emotional state that influenced those decisions.

The reason is simple. Trading generates enormous amounts of data, but human memory is selective and self-serving. You remember your best wins and forget the sloppy entries. You recall the one time you held through a drawdown and it worked, but you blur out the five times it did not. A journal forces honesty and creates the raw material for genuine improvement.

What to Track in Every Trade

A useful journal captures both the objective facts and the subjective context of each trade. Here is the complete list of fields that professional traders record:

The Objective Data

  • Date and time of entry and exit
  • Instrument traded (ES, NQ, EUR/USD, etc.)
  • Direction — long or short
  • Position size — number of contracts or lots
  • Entry price and the rationale for that specific level
  • Stop loss — where it was placed and whether it was moved
  • Target price — initial target and any adjustments
  • Exit price and exit reason (target hit, stop hit, manual close, time-based)
  • Commission and slippage
  • Net PnL on the trade

The Subjective Context

  • Market conditions: Was it trending, ranging, volatile, or quiet? Was there a major news event approaching?
  • Setup type: What pattern or signal triggered the entry? Name your setups consistently so you can analyze which ones perform best.
  • Confidence level: Rate 1-10 how confident you felt about the trade before entering.
  • Emotional state: Were you calm, anxious, revenge-trading, euphoric, bored? Be brutally honest.
  • Execution quality: Did you follow your plan? Rate 1-10.
  • Screenshot: Capture the chart at entry and exit. This is invaluable for later review.

Digital vs Paper Journals

Both approaches have merit and the best choice depends on your personality and workflow.

Paper Journals

Writing by hand forces slower, more deliberate reflection. Research in learning science consistently shows that handwriting improves retention and comprehension compared to typing. A physical notebook next to your trading station serves as a constant visual reminder to be disciplined. The downside is obvious: paper is not searchable, hard to analyze statistically, and easy to lose.

Digital Spreadsheets

A well-structured Google Sheet or Excel workbook gives you the best of both worlds. You can log trades quickly, build formulas for automatic PnL calculations, create pivot tables to analyze performance by setup type, time of day, or market condition, and generate charts that reveal patterns over time. Templates are widely available, or you can build your own.

Dedicated Journaling Software

Tools like Tradervue, Edgewonk, and TradeZella offer automatic trade import from brokers, tagging systems, and built-in analytics. They save time on data entry but add a subscription cost. For traders who find manual entry tedious, these tools significantly increase the likelihood of actually maintaining the habit.

The Weekly Review Process

A journal without review is just a diary. The real value comes from systematic weekly analysis. Block 30 to 60 minutes every weekend for this process.

Step 1: The Numbers

Review your PnL for the week. Calculate your win rate, average win, average loss, and profit factor for those five days. Compare these to your running averages. Are you improving or declining?

Step 2: Pattern Recognition

Look for recurring themes. Did your best trades share a common setup type? Did your losses cluster around a specific time of day or market condition? Were you more profitable on trending days or ranging days? These patterns are invisible in the moment but obvious in review.

Step 3: Emotional Audit

Review the emotional state entries. Correlate them with results. Most traders discover that their worst trades happen when they logged negative emotional states — frustration, boredom, or the urge to make back losses. This awareness is the first step toward breaking destructive patterns.

Step 4: CLIMB Score Check

Cross-reference your journal with your Climber Trade dashboard. Which metrics improved this week? Which declined? Can you trace the metric movements back to specific trades in your journal? If your Lot Stability Ratio dropped, was it because you took an unusually large position on Thursday? If your ABS_NPL score improved, was it because you followed the reduced-size approach on the volatile CPI day?

Step 5: Next Week's Plan

Based on your review, identify one to three specific improvements for the coming week. Keep it focused. Trying to fix everything at once fixes nothing. Maybe this week you focus on not trading during the first 15 minutes of the session, or you commit to cutting position size on Fridays when your journal shows consistently poor Friday performance.

Journal Templates for Funded Traders

If you are starting from scratch, here is a simple structure you can adapt:

  1. Pre-market plan (5 minutes): Write down the day's key levels, scheduled events, and your plan for the session. Note your mental state before trading begins.
  2. Per-trade log (during session): For each trade, record the objective data listed above plus a one-sentence rationale and your confidence level.
  3. End-of-day summary (10 minutes): Total PnL, best trade, worst trade, one thing done well, one thing to improve. Check your CLIMB Score on the dashboard.
  4. Weekly review (30-60 minutes): Follow the five-step process above. Write down your focus areas for next week.

Making the Habit Stick

The hardest part of journaling is not knowing what to write — it is doing it consistently. Here are proven strategies:

  • Start small. Even three fields per trade is better than nothing. You can expand later once the habit is established.
  • Attach it to an existing routine. Journal right after your last trade of the day, before you close your platform. Make it part of the shutdown process.
  • Do not skip losing days. The temptation to avoid journaling after a bad session is strong. Those are the most valuable entries you will ever write.
  • Review your entries. A journal you never re-read is a waste of time. The weekly review gives purpose to the daily entries.
