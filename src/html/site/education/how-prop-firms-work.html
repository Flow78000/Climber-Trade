How Prop Firms Actually Work
The Truth Behind Funded Trading

Published March 14, 2026 9 min read

The prop trading industry generates billions in annual volume, yet most retail traders have a fuzzy understanding of what actually happens behind the scenes. They know they pay an evaluation fee, they know they trade simulated capital, and they know that somewhere along the line they might receive real money. But the mechanics between those steps remain opaque, and that opacity is exactly what bad actors exploit.

This article pulls back the curtain. By the end, you will understand every revenue stream, every cost center, and every incentive that drives a prop firm's decisions.

Revenue Stream #1: Evaluation Fees

Every modern prop firm charges traders for access to an evaluation. At Climber Trade, this is $199 per month for the Standard plan or $49 for a 30-day Discovery trial. This is the most visible revenue source and, for many firms, the primary one.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: some firms are structured so that evaluation fees alone sustain the entire business. They do not need traders to pass. They do not need to allocate real capital. The evaluation itself is the product, and the promise of funding is simply the marketing angle that drives subscriptions.

This business model creates a perverse incentive. The firm earns the most money when the most traders fail and re-subscribe. Rules are calibrated to maximize failure rate while maintaining the appearance of fairness. Tight daily loss limits, mandatory profit targets within fixed windows, and reset fees all serve this purpose.

Revenue Stream #2: Profit Splits from Funded Traders

Firms that actually deploy real capital have a second, more sustainable revenue stream: their share of funded traders' profits. If a trader earns $10,000 in a month with a 50/50 split, the firm receives $5,000. This only works if the firm has real capital at risk and genuine funded accounts.

The alignment is obvious. The firm makes more money when traders make more money. This is why Climber Trade operates a transparent liquidity pool model where the capital is visible to everyone. When the funding pool is public, the firm cannot pretend to have capital it does not possess.

Where Does the Capital Come From?

This is the question that separates legitimate firms from facades. There are several models:

  • Firm's own capital. The founders or investors deposit capital that is directly traded by funded traders. This is the purest model and carries the most risk for the firm.
  • Pooled funding model. A portion of evaluation fees and profit-split revenue is continuously reinvested into a shared capital pool. Climber Trade uses this approach: 50% of subscription revenue and 50% of the firm's profit-share commission flow back into the pool.
  • Third-party investors. Some firms connect traders with external investors, similar to Darwinex's model. The firm acts as a marketplace rather than a capital provider.
  • No real capital. The firm never actually funds anyone. Simulated accounts are relabeled as "funded" accounts, and payouts come from new subscribers' fees. This is effectively a Ponzi structure and eventually collapses.

When evaluating a firm, the single most important question is: where is the capital, and can I verify it? A public liquidity pool dashboard answers this question definitively.

The Cost Structure of Running a Prop Firm

Operating a legitimate prop firm involves significant costs that traders rarely consider:

  • Platform and data feeds. Real-time market data licenses, execution infrastructure, and server hosting cost tens of thousands per month.
  • Risk management systems. Automated monitoring of every funded account requires sophisticated software and dedicated personnel.
  • Compliance and legal. KYC verification, regulatory compliance, contract management, and payment processing add substantial overhead.
  • Capital at risk. When a funded trader loses money, the firm absorbs the loss. This is the cost that firms without real capital conveniently avoid.
  • Support and operations. Customer service, dispute resolution, and technical support require a team.

These costs explain why evaluation fees exist. They also explain why some firms cut corners by never actually deploying capital. Running a real prop firm is expensive. Pretending to run one is cheap.

How Profit Splits Are Determined

Profit splits vary widely across the industry. The common range is 50/50 to 90/10 in favor of the trader. But the number alone is misleading without context.

A firm offering 90/10 splits but with a $50,000 account cap is less valuable than a firm offering 55/45 on a $500,000 allocation. The absolute dollar amount matters more than the percentage. Similarly, a firm that offers 80/20 but resets your account after any losing month is less valuable than one that uses dynamic splits tied to your ongoing performance score.

At Climber Trade, splits range from 50/50 at the Standard tier (score 85-89) to 60/40 at Elite (score 95-100). The split improves as your score improves, creating continuous incentive to trade better, not just profitably.

The Evaluation Phase: What Actually Happens

During evaluation, you trade a simulated account. Your orders execute against real market data, but no actual money changes hands in the market. The firm is measuring your behavior patterns: how you manage risk, how consistent your returns are, how you respond to drawdowns, and whether your execution quality suggests a sustainable edge.

This is fundamentally different from a live trading account, and good firms acknowledge this. The psychological pressure of simulated trading is lower, which means evaluation results tend to overstate real-world performance. Score-based systems account for this by requiring sustained performance over extended periods, typically 30 days minimum with additional confidence bonuses at 120 and 180 days.

What Happens After You Get Funded

Once you qualify, the firm allocates capital based on your score and the available pool. Your capital allocation is not a fixed number promised in advance. It is calculated using a formula that accounts for the total pool size, your score relative to other funded traders, and protective factors that prevent over-allocation.

You then trade with real capital. Your profits are real, your losses reduce the pool, and your performance continues to be monitored. If your score drops below maintenance thresholds, your allocation shrinks or pauses. If your score improves, your allocation grows. This dynamic system ensures that capital flows toward the traders who manage it best.

How to Evaluate a Prop Firm's Legitimacy

Before committing to any firm, verify these five things:

  1. Proof of real capital deployment. Is there a public pool or auditable record?
  2. Payment history. Do funded traders receive payouts? Check independent reviews and community feedback, not just the firm's testimonials.
  3. Clear rules with no hidden clauses. Read the trading rules and contract before paying anything.
  4. Aligned incentives. Does the firm profit primarily from your success or from your failure and re-subscription?
  5. Responsive support. Can you reach a human being? How fast do they respond?

The firms that survive long-term are the ones that build genuine alignment between their revenue and their traders' success. Everything else is marketing.
